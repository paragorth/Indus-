ANSWER
1. The sign-level uniformity of Indus seals (x1.04-1.10) does NOT require central issuance. Two independent modern naming populations
   (US given names, US surnames) sampled ~600 km apart give the same multiple at the same noise level, and a centrally issued code
   can be far less uniform (call signs with a district digit, x1.9-12). Uniformity of the inventory = one naming / writing
   population across two cities, which is what a shared language and a shared name stock produce without any issuing office.
2. Indus is tighter than an ancient multi-ethnic empire (Latin provinces x1.24-1.32) and than every administered multi-city
   script in the library (Ur III, Linear B); it matches only the most homogeneous modern naming populations (20th-century USA)
   and central codes without a geographic field.
3. The one feature no naming population shows is whole-text reuse below 1. Real names are always somewhat local (x1.05-3.4,
   0 of 225 pairs below 0.88). If seal texts were personal names drawn from one population, MD-H reuse should be >= 1; it is
   0.77-0.88 at every merge level. That feature, not the inventory uniformity, is the residual argument for texts supplied from
   a shared stock (formulae, offices or titles copied across cities rather than names coined locally). Its strength is limited:
   the reuse rates are 2-3%, the Indus P for locality is 0.7-0.8 (no test of 'more shared than within' is significant beyond the
   draw-bootstrap CI), and it rests on Mohenjo-daro + Harappa only.

VERDICT: the inventory-level uniformity is what one naming population across two cities looks like; central issuance is not
required to explain it. The whole-text reuse inversion remains unexplained by any name population and is the only signal that
points beyond 'one population' toward shared stock texts. Data caution: Indus counts from merged-corpus-canonical.json (older
build, S-DARK-23), seals only; FCC licensees are not a random population sample; SSA drops names < 5 births per state-year.
