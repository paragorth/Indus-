# Gods, myths and scenes: what they can and cannot anchor (25 Sept 2026)

The idea: myths travel, and Indus narrative scenes are inscribed. If a scene shows a known myth and its text names the god, that pairs an image with a name, the way Egyptian cartouches paired kings with names.

## 1. Scenes in the corpus (IM77 field symbols 470–810)

39 scene types, most seen once. Recurring: man seated in a tree with a tiger below (6 lines, plus 3 multi-scene tablets), horned bovine archer "Divine Archer" (6), horned seated figure (4+2), tiger-strangler (3), pipal-tree deity with kneeling worshipper and seven figures (5 lines), spearing of bull or buffalo (3).

**Test** (distinct texts only, one-sided Fisher, BH-FDR 5% over 35 tests): do signs recur across *different* scenes of one theme (tiger myths, horned seated figure, horned archer/bovine, pipal deity, bull scenes, processions)? **No.** The only survivors come from one caption and its variant, repeated within a single scene type (below). Before deduplication, 7 "hits" appeared, all produced by mass-produced moulded tablets.

Each moulded scene carries **one fixed text**, like the copper tablets, where each motif has one label (see `docs/`). If these texts name what the picture shows, they are the most direct image-to-name pairs in the script. Structure: the archer caption contains the tree (M169) and crescent (M287) signs before the jar (M342), and the tiger-tablet caption contains the leaf (M328) and comb (M176), so both are built from ordinary grammatical parts, not unique "name" signs.

All of these are resemblances of image to image. None supplies a *name in a known language* tied to the Indus text, and most of the Indian parallels are 1,000–2,500 years later.

## 4. The one parallel with a testable linguistic claim: Parpola's fish = star

Dravidian *mīn* means both "fish" and "star" (Old Tamil). Parpola reads number + fish as star groups: *aṟu-mīn* "six stars" = Pleiades; *mu-mīn* "three stars" = Mṛgaśiras; *ēḻ-mīn* "seven stars" = Ursa Major ([Parpola 2010](https://tamilnet.com/img/publish/2014/11/Parpola-2010-Coimbatore.pdf)).

Against our frozen data (prereg Q4, `PREREG_RESULTS.md`): short-stroke numbers before the plain fish are **3 (18), 6 (14), 4 (5), 7 (1), never 5**. Before the tree they form a smooth count (3:34, 4:42, 5:16, 6:16, 7:9, 8:3).
- **Consistent with the hypothesis:** the fish takes a few fixed numbers, which is what named star groups would look like, and 3 and 6 are the two numbers with attested Old Tamil star names.
- **Not proof:** fixed "number + fish" words could equally be clan, deity or product names. 7-fish, the Ursa Major name, is almost absent (1). 4-fish has no attested star name. The seven-figure "Pleiades" seal (text 2430: 229 219 70 342 1 242) contains no fish sign.
- **What would test it from outside:** the frequency of these compounds should follow ritual calendars. For example, 6-fish (Pleiades) should cluster on objects from contexts dated to a Pleiades-linked season. There is no dated-context data to do this.

## Verdict

Myth parallels give *hypotheses about meaning* (a lord of animals, an archer god, a tiger myth, star names). They give no sound values, so they can't pass the blind validator, and every proposed god is known only from sources centuries later. The one internal fact they fit is the fixed number + fish system (Q4), and it fits other readings equally well. The best new asset from this pass is the list of **fixed scene captions** (section 2). If an outside anchor ever gives the name of the archer god, the tiger myth or the serpent-flanked figure, these are the texts to check first.

## 5. What Mesopotamian myths say about Meluhha (CDLI literary composites, primary texts)
- *Curse of Agade* 48–49: "Meluhha, the people of the black land (*kur ge₆-ga*), brought up exotic goods of the foreign lands".
- *Enki and the World Order* 124–129, 219–222: Enki decrees fates for "Meluhha, Magan, Dilmun". The *magilum* boats of Meluhha carry gold and silver. **"Black land: may your trees be great trees, your forests *mes*-trees of the mountains, may their chairs grace royal palaces."**
- *Enki and Ninhursaga* 49c–e: "The land Meluhha: carnelian, desirable and precious; *mes*-wood of the plains, fine *ab-ba* wood; large boats" bring them to Dilmun. The same myth sets its paradise in Dilmun, where "the *dar*-bird does not utter its cry" (the *dar* bird is also a Meluhha import in Ur III accounts).
- A lament (CDLI Literary 000771) describes a person as "dark, of Meluhha" (*gig₂-ga me-luh-ha*).

In Mesopotamian myth, Meluhha is a far-off "black land" of great trees, carnelian, gold and boats. No Meluhhan god, hero or story is named. The myths describe the Meluhhans' goods, never their beliefs, so they give no name to set beside the Indus scene captions (section 2).
