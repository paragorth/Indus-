# v11 final: "the text is a walk through a hidden map"

Files: tools/v11_lib.py, v11_cycle1.py, v11_cycle2.py, v11_cycle2b.py, v11_cycle3.py, v11_cycle4.py, v11_cycle5.py, v11_cycle6.py;
loops/v11_cycle1.txt .. v11_cycle5.txt (loop cycle 4 = scripts cycle4 + cycle5; loop cycle 5 = script cycle6);
checkpoints and logs in data/results/v11/.
Data: ZL3b paragraph text (main), IT2a (transcription check; same pages, so agreement = transcription-robust, not replication).
Design: every score is held out (page-level 2-fold split, alternate folios). Every control corpus is poured into the exact ZL3b line layout.
Controls: planted random walks on a 20x20 grid (30% and 60% noise), on a random graph of the same degree, a one-way route, an
antipodal 'across the wheel' walk, a start-region cycle; Latin (Isidore), Italian (Manzoni), Spanish (Cervantes); words shuffled within lines.
Prior art: Timm & Schinner (vertical/positional similarity, self-citation); Schinner 2007 (random-walk statistics of tokens, a different
sense of 'walk'). No published test of word succession as motion in a learned map was found; the junction effect is ours (v6).

Plain answer: no. A low-dimensional hidden map does not explain the Voynich text better than the controls. The method
recovers a planted grid (12x chance local recovery, map advantage 1.9-2.7) and rejects a planted graph; on Voynich every map
statistic falls in the natural-language / random-graph band, lines show no diffusion, no triangle closure and no reuse of local
neighbourhoods, and the line-initial chain has no regional basis. What a 2-D model learns from Voynich word order is mostly
the junction between the last glyph of one word and the first glyph of the next, plus topic. Limits: top 300 word types only
(~40% of within-line bigrams); a very noisy map (60% noise) is caught only by the cycle-1 statistic; a map over sub-word units or over
rare words is not tested. Nothing here is a decipherment.
