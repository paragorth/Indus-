Idea: treat every near-repeated passage as manuscript witnesses of one text and collate them. Controls: Brumati's
herbal with planted medieval spelling (three scribes, opaque symbols), the same herbal clean, real 14th-15th c.
German (ReF), CATMuS medieval Latin and Italian, Latin and Hebrew; nulls: words shuffled within section, within
section and line slot, within page; unit-Markov per section; self-citation (copy-and-modify) per section.

Calibration (Grade A): the planted classes come back exactly (top 7 ops; context-anchored ranks 1-11); the reduced
alphabet raises held-out section accuracy +5 points (z 14) and erases the scribe (z -16); the stemma test finds the
planted scribes (z 15) and a hand-specific generator (z 10) but not a section-trained one; German gives genuine
medieval classes (i~y, j, v, marks, nasal stroke). The unit-Markov generator fails every test.

Verdict: the textual-critic machine works on real copying and fails cleanly on a Markov generator. Applied to the
Voynich it finds a thin layer of genuine re-used passages (Currier B) whose re-uses vary 6-8x more than any real
copied text. They vary in a small fixed set of positions (ch~sh, e-run/d ending, q-). This looks like
copy-and-modify, not copying with spelling variants. Not cracked. Nothing predicts outside the corpus.
Next stranger step: treat the varied slot (ch/sh, e-run, q-) between two mentions of the same passage as a
1-3 bit message, and test whether the sequence of these mutations across the book carries structure (order,
position, page) that a copy-and-modify generator with free mutation cannot produce.
